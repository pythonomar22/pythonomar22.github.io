Checking distillation papers

On-policy distillation is a post-training technique where a student model generates its own solutions, a teacher model scores every token of them, and the student trains on that signal. It works well and everyone uses it. It's in the recipes of a lot of modern open-source models (Qwen3, MiMo, among others), and was used in Composer 2.5 training. If you want a longer explanation of the technique itself, Thinking Machines has a good one.

Because it works, arXiv now gets a new variant of it roughly once a day. Between January and July of 2026 there were about 200 papers proposing tweaks: apply the loss only to high-entropy tokens, or only to "teachable" tokens, or weight by position, or gate by sign. Change the divergence to forward KL, or a forward/reverse mixture, or a power divergence, or a trust region. Use two teachers, or a reward-gated teacher, or the model itself with the answer in its context. Nearly every one of these papers reports beating the strongest baseline by a point or two on competition math benchmarks.

As a field that hasn’t been well defined yet, one might naturally wonder if these metrics are even meaningful.

Specifically, the question here is:

  1. Are these metrics meaningful?
  2. Are the variants tested against each other?

I tried to check and answer this myself, kind of motivated by the sheer number of papers that appeared in OPD/OPSD since the beginning of the summer.

Across papers, the median gain over the untrained student is about 10 points, and a third of those gains clear even the most pessimistic noise floor you can apply to a single benchmark. Vanilla on-policy distillation earns its place in the recipes. So to be clear, the question is only about the layer of variants on top.

Can the benchmarks measure a 1.6 point improvement?

AIME24, the most popular benchmark in this literature, has 30 problems.

If a model solves each problem with probability around 0.4, the standard deviation of its score from problem sampling alone is sqrt(0.4 * 0.6 / 30), which is about 9 points. So a 95% confidence interval on a single AIME24 score is about plus or minus 17.5 points. The typical paper is claiming a 1.6 point win inside that.

I just took every method-versus-best-baseline comparison from the 52 papers with usable tables, 372 comparisons in total, and plotted each one against its benchmark's noise floor:

94.4% of the comparisons (351 of 372) are inside the floor. If you restrict to the AIME-class benchmarks with 30 or 40 problems, it's 230 out of 230.

The obvious objection is that these comparisons are paired. Both models saw the same 30 problems, so some of the noise cancels, and the cancellation depends on how correlated the two models' per-problem outcomes are.

I swept the correlations, and so at a correlation of 0.5, 94.1% of the deltas are still inside the floor. At 0.8, it's 88.7%. Even at 0.95, which I think would be a pretty strange thing to assume about two different training runs, 74% of the claimed improvements can't be distinguished from noise.

Maybe it’s not fair to judge individual benchmarks (but I agree that there are many issues with how we test), but how about considering every benchmark together? If I pool every benchmark, then 46/52 papers cannot be rejected from the null hypothesis of being equal to their own baseline method with 95% confidence based on all of their benchmark results.

To find a difference of one standard deviation from a null of zero difference with 80% power (median expected size), you would need around 16000 data points for each arm, i.e., the paper needs ~16k problems. AIME24 has 30. None of these considerations take seed variance into account; most papers do not report seeds, so you add an extra point or two due to seed variance if you’re testing a model with known seed noise. These lower bounds underestimate the sample sizes you’ll need if you don’t already have them.

Many benchmarks in ML/AI/LLM research that the community uses are now quite small , so this is far from unknown to the general research community, having whole papers dedicated to discussing it Miller 2024, Hochlehnert et al. 2025. It’s one thing to understand this abstractly, but something else to see the scientific literature treat it as if it did not exist : the majority of papers in this corpus report no uncertainty whatsoever, with bolded 1.6 point wins on 30 problems.

At least compare numbers between different papers?

In an initial attempt to pool scores for “the same model” across the different papers I got spreads of 50 points in some cells.

The string “Qwen3-8B” in a results table can refer to either the base pretrained checkpoint or the hybrid with thinking enabled or the hybrid with thinking disabled or the Instruct-2507 released version of the model with avg@16 or pass@16 evaluated in a standard or agentic harness . Pooling all those together and declaring the result as “irreproducibility” was simply me being careless.

So the following table presents what remains after careful attention to the models reported and the conditions for measurement and results: 62% (428 / 694) of the Qwen3 base rows in this corpus present a model string that does not specify what measurement was performed. Bare “Qwen3-8B”, with the mode for thinking not specified at all anywhere in the paper. This is all the rows with Qwen3-8B measuring on AIME24 :

Those bare rows range from 21 to 76, and three of them that appeared as outliers actually corresponded upon close inspection of the papers: the first used thinking disabled (25.4), the second an 8k generation cap (38.3), the third a 16k generation cap (58.1). The three numbers are correct according to the source material. Just not directly comparable to the others’ tables, and this is not indicated anywhere. 42 of 63 papers do not indicate whether thinking mode was enabled. Only 12 out of 63 fully specify their sampling parameters in the main text.

Conversely, if the name that is printed refers to a specific checkpoint, then the numbers are correct, agreeing within roughly a point with each other (std 0.9 to 1.5 across labs): For the ones specifying the thinking mode, the performance for Qwen3-8B on AIME24 ranges from 74.2 to 76.5 across five papers. If we restrict our attention to the cases where everything carefully disclosed across papers are the same, the residual cross lab spread becomes about 0.6 to 2.3 points depending on the table cell.

Do different variants compete against each other?

45 of 63 papers I reviewed mentions at least one competing 2026 variant in their related work section. Some explicitly mention one as “the closest prior work.” Yet when they compare them in their result tables, these opponents turn into the same 2024 epoch baselines that every other paper compares against: SFT, GKD, vanilla OPD, SDPO, GRPO.
